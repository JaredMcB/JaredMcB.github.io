How to get a computer to solve mathematical problems approximately, and how to know whether to trust the answer: root-finding, interpolation, differentiation, integration, differential equations, and linear systems.

## About the course

Numerical analysis surveys methods for approximating solutions to problems that cannot be solved exactly. For each algorithm we care about three things: where it comes from, where it breaks down, and how efficient it is.

This is a scientific computing course as much as a mathematics course. Our language is Julia, run in Jupyter notebooks, and no prior Julia experience is assumed. Homework is light, with a written part and a short notebook. Projects are the heart of the course: code projects on root-finding and on a topic of the student's choosing, and a final project, a parameter-optimization problem built on an initial-value problem, documented in a self-contained notebook and presented.

In Fall 2026 the course runs as an independent study with a weekly check-in.

**Textbook:** *Numerical Analysis*, 9th edition, by Richard L. Burden and J. Douglas Faires.

**Prerequisites:** MAT 343: Linear Algebra and MAT 344: Elementary Differential Equations.

## Helpful resources

- [Julia](https://julialang.org/) and the [Julia documentation](https://docs.julialang.org/en/v1/). Free and open source. The manual's "Getting Started" and "Functions" sections cover most of what we use.
- [Jupyter](https://jupyter.org/). The notebook environment we work in.
- [*Fundamentals of Numerical Computation* (Julia edition)](https://tobydriscoll.net/fnc-julia/) by Driscoll and Braun. A free online textbook covering the same topics with working Julia code. A good second explanation when Burden and Faires is terse.
